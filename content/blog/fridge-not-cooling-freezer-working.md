The milk is warm, the salad is wilting — but the ice cream in the freezer is still rock solid. If that sounds familiar, you're not alone. A fridge that's stopped cooling while the freezer carries on working is one of the most common fridge freezer faults, and it has a very specific set of causes.

Here's why it happens, what you can check yourself, and when it's time to call in an engineer.

## Why the freezer works but the fridge doesn't

Most modern fridge freezers are **frost-free**. They don't have a separate cooling system for the fridge. Instead, there's a single cold coil (the evaporator) in the freezer, and a small fan blows cold air from the freezer into the fridge through a duct, controlled by a flap called a **damper**.

So if anything stops that cold air reaching the fridge, the freezer keeps freezing while the fridge slowly warms up. The usual suspects are:

- **Ice blocking the evaporator or the air duct** — usually because the automatic defrost system has a fault.
- **A failed evaporator fan** — no fan, no airflow.
- **A stuck or faulty damper** — the flap stays shut.
- **A faulty fridge temperature sensor or control board** — the fridge never asks for cold air.

On older, non-frost-free models with a separate fridge cooling plate, the causes are different — often a failed thermostat or a problem with the plate itself.

## First: protect your food

Keep the doors closed as much as possible. A fridge thermometer is the easiest way to know where you stand: food safety guidance is to keep a fridge between **0°C and 5°C**.

If high-risk food — meat, fish, dairy, cooked rice and leftovers — has been warm for several hours, the safe choice is to throw it away. When in doubt, throw it out. Move what you can to a cool box or a neighbour's fridge while you sort the problem out.

## Checks you can do yourself

### 1. The temperature setting

Check the fridge setting hasn't been knocked or changed — it happens more often than you'd think, especially with touch controls. Some models also have a "holiday" or "vacation" mode that lets the fridge warm up deliberately.

### 2. Blocked vents inside the fridge

Look for the air outlet, usually at the top or back of the fridge compartment. If it's blocked by a tall bottle, a packed shelf or a bag of salad pressed against it, cold air can't get in. Clear the space around it and give it a few hours.

### 3. Door seals

Warm, humid air leaking in through a split or loose seal causes more frost in the freezer and makes the fridge work harder. Try the paper test: close the door on a sheet of paper. If it slides out with no resistance, the seal isn't doing its job.

### 4. Space to breathe

Fridge freezers get rid of heat through the back or underneath. If yours is squeezed into a tight alcove, pushed hard against the wall or surrounded by clutter, it can struggle — particularly in summer. Make sure there's a gap behind and above, and vacuum any dust off the coils at the back.

### 5. Listen for the fan

Open the freezer and listen. On many models you'll hear the fan running when the appliance is cooling (some pause the fan when the door is open — try pressing the door switch with your finger). A **grinding or ticking** noise often means ice is catching the fan blades. **Silence** can mean the fan has failed.

### 6. The manual defrost test

If you suspect ice is the problem, a manual defrost is a useful test:

1. Move your food somewhere cold.
2. Switch the fridge freezer off at the wall and leave both doors open for **12 to 24 hours**, with towels on the floor to catch water.
3. Switch it back on and give it several hours to come down to temperature.

If the fridge cools properly again, an ice blockage was the cause. But if the problem comes back within days or weeks, the **automatic defrost system has a fault** — usually the defrost heater, a defrost sensor or the timer/control board — and the ice will keep returning until it's repaired.

## When to call an engineer

It's worth booking a repair if:

- The fridge stays warm even after a manual defrost.
- It warms up again within days or weeks of defrosting (a defrost fault).
- The evaporator fan is silent, or you can hear it grinding.
- You see an error code or a flashing temperature display.
- The compressor (the black unit at the back, near the bottom) is clicking on and off repeatedly or isn't running at all.

Fans, defrost heaters, sensors and dampers are usually well worth replacing. Faults in the sealed refrigeration system — such as a refrigerant leak or a failed compressor — are a bigger job, and on an older or cheaper appliance a replacement may make more sense. A good engineer will tell you which side of that line you're on before any work is done.

## Keeping your fridge freezer healthy

- Leave a gap around the appliance for airflow, and vacuum the coils once or twice a year.
- Let leftovers cool before putting them in.
- Don't overfill shelves or block the air vents.
- Check door seals now and then, and wipe them clean — sticky seals tear more easily.
- Keep a fridge thermometer inside so you notice problems early.
