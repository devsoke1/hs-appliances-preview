When a commercial fridge starts running warm, the clock is ticking on your stock, your food safety records and — if it's a customer-facing display — your sales. Whether it's a cold room in a restaurant kitchen, a dairy cabinet in a convenience store or a back-bar cooler in a pub, the steps below will help you protect your stock, rule out the simple causes and give an engineer the information they need.

## The temperatures that matter

- **Chilled food:** in England, the law requires chilled food to be kept at **8°C or below**. Most businesses aim for **5°C or lower** to give themselves a safety margin.
- **Frozen food:** freezers and freezer rooms typically run at **-18°C or colder**.

Your own food safety management system (for many small businesses that's the FSA's *Safer Food, Better Business* pack) should set out your target temperatures and what to do when something goes wrong. Follow it.

## Step 1: Act straight away

1. **Record the temperature and the time.** Check the unit's display and, if you can, use a probe thermometer to check the temperature of the food itself — air temperature rises and falls faster than product temperature.
2. **Keep doors closed.** Every opening lets more warm air in.
3. **Move high-risk stock** — cooked meats, dairy, fish, prepared foods — to working refrigeration if you have it.
4. **Decide what to do with stock that's been too warm** in line with your food safety procedures. If you're unsure, don't sell it.
5. **Write down what you did.** A clear record protects you if an environmental health officer asks later.

## Step 2: Quick checks staff can make

These checks are safe for staff to do and solve a surprising number of "breakdowns".

### Power and controller

- Is the unit switched on and the display lit?
- Has the **set point been changed** — accidentally or by someone trying to "turn it up"?
- Is there an **alarm or error code** on the controller? Note it down or take a photo.

### Doors and seals

- Has a door been **propped open** or left ajar during a delivery or a busy service?
- Are door **gaskets split, flattened or hanging loose**? Do doors close and seal by themselves?
- On freezer rooms, is there **ice around the door frame**? That can mean a failed frame heater.

### Loading and airflow

- **Open multideck cabinets** rely on a curtain of cold air down the front. Stock stacked past the **load line**, or packaging hanging over shelf edges, breaks that curtain.
- **Cold rooms and reach-in fridges** need space for air to circulate. Boxes stacked tight against the evaporator (the fan unit) choke the airflow.
- Have you just loaded a large delivery or **warm product**? A unit can take hours to pull back down.

### Heat around the unit

- Is the **condenser** — the coil and fan that get rid of heat, usually behind a grille at the bottom or on an outdoor unit — clogged with dust or kitchen grease?
- Has something been put in front of the vents, or is the unit now next to an oven, a glasswasher or in direct sun?
- For cold rooms and cellar cooling, is the **outdoor condensing unit** clear of leaves, rubbish and stacked crates?

### Ice and water

- Is there **heavy ice** on the evaporator coil? That usually points to a defrost problem or warm, humid air getting in.
- Is water **pooling inside or leaking** onto the floor? A blocked drain line is common — and a slip hazard. Put a sign out and mop up.

## Step 3: When to call an engineer

Call for a repair if, after those checks:

- The temperature keeps rising, or won't come back down within a reasonable time.
- The compressor isn't running, or is starting and stopping every few minutes.
- Fans aren't turning, or are noisy.
- The evaporator keeps icing up.
- You notice **oily marks** on pipework (a possible sign of a refrigerant leak).
- The controller shows an alarm you can't clear.

Handling refrigerant gases legally requires certified engineers, so leave anything involving the sealed system to a professional.

To help the engineer, have ready: the **make and model** (on the rating plate inside the unit or on the back), the **temperatures you've recorded**, any **error codes**, and when the problem started.

## Preventing the next breakdown

Most commercial refrigeration failures build up slowly. A few habits make a real difference:

- **Log temperatures at least twice a day** — you'll spot a unit that's struggling before it fails.
- **Clean condenser coils regularly**, more often in kitchens where grease builds up quickly.
- **Respect load lines** and keep space around evaporators.
- **Check door gaskets** weekly and replace damaged ones promptly — they're cheap compared with lost stock and energy.
- **Keep outdoor units clear**, especially before summer.
- Consider **planned maintenance**: periodic professional checks of coils, fans, defrost operation, controllers and electrical connections catch problems early.
